    What Insurer Steering Is and How to Handle It

    Insurer steering is a practice in which an insurance company uses its communication with policyholders to direct them toward specific repair shops rather than allowing them to freely exercise their legal right to choose. In auto glass claims, steering is a common experience for Pennsylvania drivers. Understanding what steering looks like, why it happens, and how to respond effectively protects your rights and ensures you receive service from a shop you actually want working on your vehicle.

    Why Steering Happens

    Insurance carriers develop networks of preferred auto glass shops through volume agreements. Shops in these networks commit to the insurer's pricing structure, which is typically lower than the shop's standard retail pricing, in exchange for being recommended to the insurer's policyholders. This arrangement reduces the insurer's claims costs.

    The financial incentive for insurers to steer policyholders toward preferred shops is real and significant. A network shop may be authorized to charge the insurer 10 to 20 percent less than a non-network shop for equivalent work. Across tens of thousands of claims, that difference represents substantial savings. Steering policyholders toward preferred shops is one of the primary mechanisms through which insurers capture these savings.

    What Steering Looks Like

    Steering can be direct or subtle. Recognizing both forms helps you respond appropriately:

    Direct steering involves explicit language suggesting your choices are limited:

    • "You must use one of our approved shops for this claim to be processed."
    • "We can only authorize work at shops in our network."
    • "Your policy requires the use of our preferred providers."

    These statements are false as a matter of Pennsylvania law. They imply a requirement that does not exist in your policy and do not accurately represent your rights.

    Subtle steering uses language that discourages non-network choice without explicitly claiming it is forbidden:

    • "Our preferred shops are the most convenient and handle all the paperwork."
    • "If you use another shop, you may need to pay upfront and get reimbursed."
    • "We cannot guarantee the quality of work at shops outside our network."
    • "Our network shops offer a warranty backed by us; others do not."

    These statements are not necessarily false, but they frame the choice in a way designed to make using a non-network shop seem burdensome or risky.

    How to Respond

    When you encounter any form of steering, a clear, confident response is effective:

    "I understand you have preferred shops. I am exercising my right under Pennsylvania law to use the shop of my choice. Please process my claim through [shop name]. My claim number is [number]. Please confirm you will work directly with the shop I have chosen."

    This response is direct, references your legal right, and moves the conversation toward action rather than argument. Most claims representatives will process a claim through a non-network shop without significant resistance when the policyholder is clear about their choice. The steering often relies on policyholders not knowing they have the right to push back.

    If the Insurer Refuses to Work with Your Chosen Shop

    Outright refusal to process a claim through a non-network shop is a rare but serious situation. If an insurer refuses to work with a qualified auto glass shop based solely on the shop's network status, options include:

    • Request a written explanation of why the claim cannot be processed through your chosen shop
    • Ask to speak with a supervisor or the claims department manager
    • File a complaint with the Pennsylvania Insurance Department, which enforces regulations protecting consumers' rights in claims handling
    • Contact a licensed public adjuster or attorney who specializes in insurance disputes if the situation warrants it

    In practice, most steering situations resolve at the first step: stating clearly that you are choosing a specific shop and asking the insurer to work with them. Insurer representatives typically know that refusing to process a legitimate claim over shop choice is not a defensible position.
